About Laurie

I am a current postdoctoral researcher at the University of British Columbia in the King Lab. I explore interactions between eco-evolutionary dynamics and management using quantitative methods, thinking about two major questions: (1) "how might populations respond to global change via adaptation, and how do evolutionary responses interact with ecological responses to change?" and (2) “how can managers account for and leverage ongoing evolutionary processes (i.e., evolutionary-enlightened management) to achieve their goals?”. My work speaks to fundamental questions in biology by quantifying how selection, gene flow, and other evolutionary forces affect population dynamics and persistence in human-modified environments. Additionally, addressing these questions helps anticipate possible trade-offs between evolutionary and ecological responses of populations to global change and management interventions. I have predominantly addressed these questions in marine systems.

Before coming to UBC, I obtained my PhD in Population Biology at UC Davis under the supervision of Marissa Baskett. I completed my undergraduate degree in Biology and Mathematics at St. Olaf College.

Research

I have studied a range of questions related to management and eco-evolutionary dyanmics. Below are some of my current projects, predominantly inspired by marine and disease systems. I am always excited to chat with people about human-induced evolution, disease dyanmics and potential collaborations!

Evolution to avoid extinction: Populations facing global change can move to new locations, acclimate in place through plasticity, or evolve—in the absence of these, the population goes extinct. I am interested in how populations can use these different strategies to avoid extinction in the face of global change, with a particular emphasis on evolutionary responses on short time scales that allow persistence (“evolutionary rescue”). Working with a team of collaborators, I collated examples of evolutionary rescue in the wild, writing a review in Trends in Ecology and Evolution. Our synthesis demonstrates that evolutionary rescue is possible in wild populations, including in conservation contexts where evolutionary rescue has traditionally been dismissed as unrealistic and unlikely (see coverage by the Wildlife Society).

Complementing this synthesis, I developed an individual based model of evolutionary rescue in host-pathogen systems to identify the ecological and evolutionary ingredients that most facilitate rescue, now published at PNAS. As part of this work, I showed that evolutionary rescue is more likely when populations face novel diseases compared to abiotic stress that affect all individuals in the population simultaneously and permanently, conditioned on genetic architecture and allele qualities.

Management and conservation: I aim to produce research that helps identify big-picture, strategic management insights and provides guidance for conservation and management. For example, I led a team of Canadian and US researchers in identifying evolutionarily informed pest-management strategies for salmon aquaculture. I used evolutionary invasion modeling combined with an economics framework to show that effective treatment of aquaculture pests can jointly achieve conservation and economic goals, but results in increased (undesirable) evolution of treatment resistance, published in Theoretical Ecology. This and similar projects link specific aquaculture practices to tradeoffs between conservation, economic and evolutionary outcomes, demonstrating the value of, and pathways towards, evolutionary informed management for salmon aquaculture that avoids negative impacts of evolution.

Diseases in marine species: A special interest of mine is disease dynamics in marine systems. With collaborators at NOAA, I developed a spatio-temporal statistical model to understand patterns of bitter crab disease, a fatal disease infecting snow crab in the Eastern Bering Sea, published in ICES Journal of Marine Science. Our work can help to identify future risk of disease outbreaks and point potential management interventions (and their limitations). As a postdoctoral researcher, I am using mathematical models to understand how environmental stress affect disease dynamics and evolutionary emergence, with applications to arctic marine mammal populations.
